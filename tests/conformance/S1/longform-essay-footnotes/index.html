The Unglamorous Art of Keeping Things Running

We celebrate the people who build bridges. We rarely notice the people who stop them from falling down.

By Ines Halvorsen · 14 min read

Every weekday morning at a quarter past five, a crew of four leaves a depot on the east bank of the river in a white van with a ladder on the roof. They are not going anywhere in particular. Their job, as their supervisor Walter Mbeki puts it, is "to go and look at things." They look at expansion joints and drainage grates, at the paint on railings and the bolts on lamp posts. They look at the same things they looked at last month, and the month before that, and in the great majority of cases they find nothing wrong.

That is the point. The city of Harwick spends a little under £3.1 million a year on this kind of routine inspection across its 212 bridges, culverts and footways.1 It is the least interesting line in the transport budget, and every councillor I spoke to could recite, unprompted, a list of more exciting things it could pay for.

I want to make the case that it is also the most important line, and that the way we talk about it—or, more often, fail to talk about it—tells us something uncomfortable about how societies decide what is worth doing.

The ribbon and the spanner

New things have a constituency. A new bridge comes with a ribbon, a ceremony, a photograph of a smiling official holding oversized scissors. It appears in a manifesto and, later, in a list of achievements. Nobody has ever held a ceremony to mark the fifteenth consecutive year in which a bridge did not collapse.

Nobody holds a ceremony for the fifteenth year a bridge did not collapse.

Economists have a dry name for this imbalance. They call maintenance a credence good from the point of view of the voter: its quality cannot be judged even after it has been bought. You cannot see a well-maintained drain. You can only see a flooded street, and by the time you see it the decision that caused it was made years earlier by someone who has since moved on.

The result is a steady drift. In one study of forty mid-sized councils, the share of capital budgets spent on renewal and repair fell from 46% to 31% over two decades, while spending on new schemes rose to fill the gap.2 None of the councils had decided to neglect their assets. They had simply, year after year, found a more pressing use for the money.

Walter Mbeki has watched this happen from the inside. When he joined the inspection team in 1998 there were eleven people on it. Today there are six, covering roughly a third more structures. "Nobody ever cut us," he told me. "They just never replaced anyone who left."

What deferral really costs

The standard defence of delay is that it is prudent. Money is tight; the bridge is fine for now; better to wait until the need is certain. It sounds responsible. It is usually the most expensive option available.

Engineers describe the decay of most structures with a curve that is flat for a long time and then drops sharply. A concrete deck with a small crack that lets in water will look almost unchanged for years. Then the steel inside begins to corrode, expands, and splits the concrete from within. The repair that would have cost a few thousand pounds in year three becomes a full replacement in year fifteen.

Deferral is not saving money; it is borrowing it, at a punishing rate of interest, from a future budget that will have its own emergencies to deal with. And unlike ordinary borrowing, it does not appear on any balance sheet. There is no line in the accounts for "bridge slowly getting worse".

A handful of places have tried to change this. Since 2019, the neighbouring county of Pellow has published an annual asset condition report that puts a single figure on the backlog: the cost of bringing everything back to a good state. When it was first published the figure was £94 million. It has since fallen to £71 million, one of the few declines in the country.

Once the number was printed, it stopped being an engineering problem and became a political one. Every year someone had to stand up and explain why it had gone up or down.

Ruth Adeyinka, former finance director, Pellow County Council

The people who notice

If maintenance is invisible to voters, it is anything but invisible to the people who do it. Spend a day with Mbeki's crew and you begin to see the city differently: as a vast, slowly shifting machine held together by thousands of small acts of attention.

At a footbridge over the ring road, Aisha Kowalczyk, the youngest member of the team, crouches beside a bearing and points out a streak of rust no longer than my thumb. "That's new since March," she says, photographing it and noting the reference number. It will go into a database, be assigned a priority, and—if the budget allows—be cleaned and painted before winter. If it does not, it will be photographed again in September, and the streak will be a little longer.

The work is not heroic. It is attentive, which is rarer and, in the long run, worth more.

There is a particular kind of expertise here that is hard to write down. The team know which structures "talk"—which ones creak in cold weather, which drains block every autumn, which joints were badly installed in the eighties and need watching. Much of this knowledge lives in the heads of people close to retirement. When they leave, it leaves with them.3

Some of that loss is inevitable. Some of it is a choice. Harwick has no formal programme for pairing experienced inspectors with new ones; Kowalczyk learned by following Mbeki around for two years, because he happened to be willing to teach. That is how most of the country's maintenance knowledge is passed on: informally, generously, and precariously.

Making the invisible visible

What would it take to give maintenance the status it deserves? The answers I heard fall into three broad groups.

The first is accounting. As Pellow shows, putting a number on the backlog changes the conversation. It turns a vague sense that "things are getting a bit worn" into a figure that can be tracked, compared and argued over.

The second is ring-fencing. Several councils now set aside a fixed share of their capital budget for renewal, which cannot be raided for new schemes without a formal vote. It is a blunt tool, but blunt tools are hard to quietly work around.

The third, and hardest, is culture. We could simply choose to celebrate the people who keep things working. That sounds naive, but it has precedent: hospitals track and publicise their infection rates; airlines are proud of their safety records. In both cases, the absence of disaster is treated as an achievement worth measuring. There is no reason a city could not do the same for its bridges.

On my last morning with the crew, we stopped at a small stone bridge over a stream on the outskirts of the city. It was built in 1821. It carries a lane that sees perhaps two hundred cars a day. Mbeki walked slowly along its parapet, then climbed down the bank to look underneath. He was there for nearly ten minutes.

"It's fine," he said when he came back up. "It's been fine for two hundred years." He paused. "That's because someone has been looking at it for two hundred years."
