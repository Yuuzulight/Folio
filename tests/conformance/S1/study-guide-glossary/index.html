Acids, Bases and pH

A one-evening review for the unit test on Thursday. Work through each section, then try the self-check questions without looking at the answers.

By the end you should be able to

  • Define acids and bases using the Brønsted–Lowry model.
  • Calculate pH from [H+] and the reverse.
  • Explain why a buffer resists changes in pH.

1 What makes an acid an acid?

In the Brønsted–Lowry model, an acid is a proton donor and a base is a proton acceptor. When hydrogen chloride dissolves in water, it gives a proton (H+) to a water molecule, forming the hydronium ion:

HCl + H2O → H3O+ + Cl−

Every acid has a conjugate base: whatever is left after it loses its proton. Here, Cl− is the conjugate base of HCl. Likewise, H3O+ is the conjugate acid of water.

Strong versus weak

A strong acid ionises completely in water; a weak acid only partly does. Strength is about how much the acid ionises, not how concentrated the solution is.

Common mistake "Strong" does not mean "concentrated". A 0.001 M solution of HCl is dilute but still a strong acid; a 5 M solution of ethanoic acid is concentrated but still weak.

2 The pH scale

pH is defined as the negative base-10 logarithm of the hydrogen ion concentration:

pH = −log10[H+]

Because the scale is logarithmic, each step of 1 is a tenfold change. A solution at pH 3 has 102 = 100 times more H+ than one at pH 5.

Worked example

A solution has [H+] = 3.2 × 10−4 mol dm−3. Find its pH.

  1. Write the formula: pH = −log10(3.2 × 10−4).
  2. Evaluate the log: log10(3.2 × 10−4) ≈ −3.49.
  3. Change the sign: pH ≈ 3.49.
Remember At 25 °C, pH + pOH = 14. If you are given [OH−], find the pOH first, then subtract from 14.

3 Buffers

A buffer is a solution of a weak acid and its conjugate base (or a weak base and its conjugate acid) in similar amounts. Add a little strong acid and the conjugate base mops up the extra H+; add a little strong base and the weak acid neutralises the OH−. Either way, the pH barely moves.

Your blood is buffered by the carbonic acid / hydrogencarbonate pair, which holds it between pH 7.35 and 7.45. The pH of a buffer can be estimated with the H–H equation:

pH = pKa + log10( [A−] / [HA] )

4 Self-check

Q1. What is the conjugate base of HNO3?

NO3−, the nitrate ion.

Q2. Solution A has pH 2 and solution B has pH 4. How many times more H+ does A contain?

104−2 = 100 times more.

Q3. Why can't a strong acid and its conjugate base form a useful buffer?

The conjugate base of a strong acid is so weak that it does not accept protons in any useful amount, so it cannot absorb added H+.

5 Glossary

Acid
A species that donates a proton (H+).
Base
A species that accepts a proton.
Conjugate pair
Two species that differ by exactly one proton, such as NH4+ and NH3.
Hydronium ion
H3O+; the form H+ takes in water.
Ka
The acid dissociation constant. A larger Ka means a stronger acid.
Buffer
A solution that resists changes in pH when small amounts of acid or base are added.
Neutralisation
The reaction of an acid with a base to form a salt and water.
